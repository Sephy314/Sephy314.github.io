## Overview

This repository is research, not an application. It asks one question — **what does leadership actually
cost in distributed consensus?** — and answers it by measuring two protocol families under the same
conditions instead of arguing from first principles.

| Protocol | Leadership model | Implementation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raft | Leader-based | `github.com/hashicorp/raft`, pinned v1.7.3 |
| Raft | Leader-based | `go.etcd.io/raft` v3.7.0, lab adapter |
| EPaxos | Leaderless / egalitarian | `efficient/epaxos`, pinned commit (CMU 2013), vendored |
| EPaxos | Leaderless / egalitarian | `nvanbenschoten/epaxos`, pinned commit, vendored |

Two implementations per protocol is the point. With one, a throughput gap cannot be attributed to the
protocol rather than to the library that happens to implement it — so each family is measured twice, and a
separate correctness harness checks that all four actually perform consistent consensus execution.

The result is two deliverables: the **Consensus Lab**, a harness that builds every implementation into
containers, drives them with one shared client, and produces the measured dataset; and the **paper**,
*The Effects of Leadership in Distributed Consensus*, written from abstract to conclusion and released as
a PDF.

## Why measure instead of argue

The case for leaderless consensus is usually a latency story: with no leader to route through, a write can
commit from wherever the client happens to be. The case against it is a contention story: conflicting
writes have to converge on the same order anyway, so the parallelism buys nothing and the coordination
costs more.

Both stories are true, under different workloads. That makes the interesting question not *which protocol
is better* but *where the crossover sits* — an empirical question with a measurable answer. The matrix
therefore varies the things the stories disagree about: replica count, workload mix, conflict rate, and
failure behaviour.

Because the comparison **is** the contribution, the lab does not touch the protocols. The upstream EPaxos
implementations are vendored at pinned commits and the Raft implementations are pinned module
dependencies; each is wrapped in a thin adapter that contains no consensus logic of its own. Anything the
lab could tune is something the results would then have to defend.

## Inside the lab

| Path | Responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| `lab/upstream/` | Vendored upstreams, pinned and never modified: `efficient/epaxos` (Apache-2.0, CMU 2013), `nvanbenschoten/epaxos`, and the Raft modules |
| `lab/adapters/` | One thin adapter per implementation — `raft`, `etcdraft`, `nvbepaxos`; wiring only, no consensus logic |
| `lab/master/raft/` | Coordination service for Raft runs — leader reporting only, no measurement logic |
| `lab/client/` | One benchmark client, one wire protocol, all four implementations |
| `lab/monitor/` | Host-side per-container resource sampler |
| `lab/runner/` | Build, smoke, correctness, and matrix entry points |
| `lab/report/` | Python processing, figure generation, HTML report |
| `lab/configs/` | Experiments as files, not as typed commands: matrix, smoke, sensitivity, commcost, persistence, networkdelay, conflictvalidation |
| `paper/` | ACM-format LaTeX sources, with `paper/release/` holding the published PDFs (English and Korean) |
| `lab/docs/`, `docs/` | Benchmark protocol, audit and reproducibility notes; methodology and experiment design |

The staging matters as much as the structure. `make smoke` runs staged checks — build, cluster bring-up,
request path, workload, injected failures, metrics — so a broken harness fails loudly before it burns
hours of matrix time producing numbers nobody can trust, and `make correctness` checks the thing the
benchmark assumes: that each implementation applies every acknowledged request exactly once.

The exclusion rule is worth calling out. Contaminated runs are not silently dropped to make a chart look
cleaner — they are counted, attributed to a host-telemetry condition, and replaced, with the count
reported. A dataset that hides its own failures cannot support a conclusion about reliability.

An implementation is only allowed to be slow for the right reason, so `make correctness` runs 18
deterministic checks per implementation family — sequential, concurrent, and under leader, follower, and
replica failures. All four pass: every replied request is applied exactly once, surviving replicas agree on
the final state, and Raft restarts recover the committed state.

## Headline results

Medians over ten independent repetitions, from the scaling family at 100 % writes and concurrency 32 —
Raft against EPaxos, in requests per second and p50 write latency:

| Replicas | Raft (req/s) | EPaxos (req/s) | EPaxos ÷ Raft | Raft p50 (ms) | EPaxos p50 (ms) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 4,351 | 5,292 | 1.22× | 6.9 | 5.6 |
| 5 | 3,150 | 5,443 | 1.73× | 9.2 | 5.5 |
| 7 | 2,590 | 3,053 | 1.18× | 12.0 | 5.4 |
| 9 | 2,113 | 2,094 | 0.99× | 14.6 | 5.5 |

Medians rather than means because a handful of repetitions stalled — two Raft runs under elevated host
load, six EPaxos runs at low and high load. The means are lower for both protocols at every replica count,
so reporting them would have described the stalls rather than the protocols.

What the wider evaluation found:

- **The leaderless advantage is real, but it does not survive scale.** EPaxos led on median throughput at
  3, 5, and 7 replicas and on median latency at every replica count — then the throughput gap closed at 9
  replicas (0.99×). Throughput fell for *both* protocols as replicas were added, by 60 % for EPaxos and
  51 % for Raft, so the scaling penalty is not specific to leader-based ordering.
- **The load range matters more than the replica count.** At concurrency 1, Raft's median was 304 req/s
  against 175 req/s for EPaxos. At concurrency 256 it inverted: 19,333 req/s against 41,077 req/s, with
  p50 latency 12.1 ms against 5.6 ms.
- **Failure cost follows the failed role, not the protocol.** Killing the Raft leader left a 1.81–4.74 s
  write-availability gap and 2,169–4,133 failed requests. Killing a Raft follower or an EPaxos replica
  stayed below 0.09 s and 106 failed requests.
- **CPU totals were similar; the shape was not.** Aggregate utilisation was 35.8 % (Raft) against 35.0 %
  (EPaxos), but in Raft the *leader* used less CPU than its followers — a leader/follower ratio of
  0.61–0.75, consistent with the followers carrying the log persistence and apply work — while EPaxos
  stayed flat at 22.4–23.0 %. Peak per-replica RSS was about 1.8× higher for EPaxos: 46.8 MB against
  26.4 MB.
- **The conflict sweep did not move throughput** — medians spanned 4,152–4,504 req/s (Raft) and
  5,079–5,392 req/s (EPaxos) — but the EPaxos fast/slow-path counters show that a large conflict fraction
  did push commands onto the slow path. *Did throughput move?* and *did contention happen?* are answered
  as separate questions instead of being conflated into one.
- **EPaxos timed out at seven and nine replicas** — 30–250 request timeouts per run at the 2,000 ms client
  timeout — which accounts for its reduced throughput under the closed-loop concurrency accounting. The
  successful requests still completed in 5.4–5.6 ms, the same as at three replicas.

## Experiments after the recorded suite

Three later families sit on top of the recorded dataset. Each configures its own experiments and writes to
its own results subtree, so the recorded dataset is never read or written by them — and each has a
`-dry-run` target that expands and validates its configuration without executing anything.

| Family | What it varies | Why it exists |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Persistence matching | Only how each implementation persists consensus state | The recorded Raft implementations persist (BoltDB, a fsynced write-ahead log) while both recorded EPaxos implementations keep state in memory, so the recorded throughput gap mixes consensus structure with a storage-path difference |
| Clean network delay | A fixed one-way latency of 0/1/3/5/10 ms, no jitter, applied with `tc/netem` in each replica's network namespace | The archived `commcost` family injected delay *inside* each implementation's send path, so it ranked injection points as well as implementations — those numbers stay a separate dataset |
| Conflict validation | The configured hot-key fraction *and* the number of distinct hot keys | Measures the *realized* conflict fraction instead of asserting the configured one, and reports the EPaxos conflict and fast/slow-path counters where the implementation provides them |

Two details carry the weight here. The persistence family uses only modes an implementation actually
provides — `nvanbenschoten/epaxos` exposes an in-memory `Storage` and no durable mode, so that cell is
skipped and reported as a gap rather than filled with a mechanism the lab would have had to write itself.
And the network-delay runner verifies the installed qdisc against the kernel, storing the commands, their
output, and that verification in the run's `network.json`: a run whose emulation did not apply fails
instead of being recorded as a delay measurement.

The archived communication-cost points are still reported as their own dataset: at a 10 ms injected cost,
HashiCorp Raft lost 72 % of its throughput, nvanbenschoten EPaxos 81 %, the original EPaxos 96 %, and etcd
Raft collapsed by 94 % already at 1 ms.

## Reproducibility

```bash
cd lab
make build              # Go binaries + upstream implementations + lab image
make smoke              # staged smoke gate before any real run
make correctness        # 18 deterministic consensus checks, all four implementations
make matrix             # the recorded experiment matrix (hours)
make report             # raw -> processed -> figures -> HTML report -> validation
make validate           # validate the report against the underlying data

make persistence-dry-run    # expand + validate a later family, run nothing
make persistence            # durable vs in-memory, four implementations, r3 + r5
make network-delay          # OS-level tc/netem delay, no jitter
make conflict-validation    # configured vs realized conflict
```

`lab/results/` is generated and deliberately not tracked by git. That is the property that makes the work
checkable: the dataset can be rebuilt from the configurations instead of trusted as an artifact, and
`make validate` re-checks the report against the data it claims to summarise. The report itself is a
single self-contained HTML file with charts embedded as base64 PNGs — it opens offline, which matters when
the point is that a reader can verify the claim without asking me for anything.

## Threats to validity

These are written down rather than discovered by a reviewer, and they bound every claim in the paper:

- **Single-machine execution** — all replicas run on one host, so results may not generalise to
  multi-machine deployments.
- **Container scheduling effects** — container overhead, CPU sharing, and network namespacing all
  influence measurements.
- **Closed-loop client** — the workload is batch-oriented, so throughput and latency are measured per
  batch, not as a precise open-loop load.
- **Approximate recovery measurement** — recovery is batch-granular, not a precise availability figure.
- **Local bridge networking** — no WAN latency; wide-area behaviour is out of scope.
- **Unmatched persistence in the recorded suite** — the recorded Raft implementations persist consensus
  state while both recorded EPaxos implementations keep it in memory, so the recorded throughput
  comparison combines consensus structure with a storage-path difference. Until the persistence-matching
  family is reported, that difference must not be read as a protocol-level effect.
- **Legacy in-adapter delay injection** — the archived `commcost` family injected delay inside each
  implementation's send path, so its ranking reflects the injection point as well as the implementation.
- **Configuration-bound availability gaps** — a measured gap is bounded by the configured failure
  detection budget (heartbeat 1000 ms, election 2000 ms) and, for election runs, by the injected isolation
  window. Gaps are reported as observed under that configuration, not as generic recovery times.
- **Resource contention is not assumed** — whether local CPU bounded the large replica counts is decided
  from recorded per-replica telemetry (the busiest replica reached 38 % and 35 % of its 2-CPU quota at 7
  and 9 replicas, with host telemetry flagging 0 of 20 runs), not from the requested quota.
- **Implementation-specific behaviour** — results reflect four specific implementations, not the protocols
  in the abstract; the sensitivity and communication-cost experiments measure how much of the difference
  is implementation-level rather than protocol-level.
- **Representative, not universal** — Raft and EPaxos stand in for leader-based and leaderless consensus;
  they do not exhaust either category.

## Status

The recorded matrix has been run and processed, the correctness harness passes for all four
implementations, and the paper is written from abstract to conclusion. The released PDF lives in
`paper/release/` — English, with a Korean translation alongside — and `paper/` rebuilds it from source.

The three later families are configured and write to their own result subtrees; the headline numbers above
are the recorded dataset and do not yet include them. Two configuration reports ship with the data:
`failure-configuration.md`, which puts the failure-detection budget beside every measured availability
gap, and `resource-contention.md`, which reports per-replica CPU saturation at each replica count.

The framing is deliberate: no claim is made about behaviour that was not measured. Every reported number
traces back to `lab/results/` under the conditions documented in `docs/experiment-design.md` — which is
what separates a benchmark from an opinion with a graph attached.
